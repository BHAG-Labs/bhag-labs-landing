Designing the BHAG Labs logo

Two circles and a leaning line. How we got there, why it matters, and the small piece of geometry that holds it together.

Most logos start with a brand brief and end with a typeface. This one started with a question instead: what shape is a launchpad?

BHAG Labs builds software for universities, accelerators, and innovation programs. The whole job is to take a founder with a Big Hairy Audacious Goal and give them the runway, the rituals, and the rigour to actually ship something. So the mark could not be a polite abstract monogram. It had to do something. It had to feel like leaning into a piece of infrastructure and finding it solid.

We spent about two weeks on this. Thirteen named versions, dozens of unnamed ones, one moment where everything clicked. Here is the whole story.

The brief, in one line

Build a mark that reads as a B at glance, holds up as geometry on closer look, and tells a story on a third look. No icon-as-decoration. No metaphor that needs a paragraph to explain.

Mission for the mark: a founder, a launchpad, and the magic that happens when their idea finds the market it was looking for.

Three starting concepts

We sketched in three directions before committing to one. Each was a different theory of what a B-mark could be.

Concept A was an aperture, a precise framed plate that read like a camera lens. Too quiet for a launchpad. Concept B was a Bauhaus B, full silhouette, big and authoritative. Too settled for a company whose entire product is helping founders move. Concept C started from a different question entirely: what if a B is just two circles and a line?

Two circles and a line is what a B literally is in classical typography. A vertical stem and two bowls. Reduce it to its geometric primitives and you get something that can scale infinitely, animate cleanly, and stay readable at favicon size. We picked C.

The iteration

Then we had to find the right two circles and the right line. This took ten versions.

v2 to v4: finding the relationship between the bowls

We started with two equal circles overlapping in a Vesica Piscis. It is geometrically perfect and visually inert. A B has unequal bowls in almost every serif type design ever drawn, with the lower bowl heavier than the upper one. So we made the lower disc larger.

The ratio settled at R / r = 60 / 48 = 5 / 4. That is close to what classical serif designers picked for their B-bowls (it is also a clean mathematical fraction, which felt right for a company whose products are mostly software). The lower bowl is exactly four-fifths heavier than the upper.

v6 to v7: discovering the lens

Then we let the discs overlap. The intersection of two circles is called a lens, and once we drew it in a separate colour, the mark stopped being a B and started being an event. Two worlds meeting.

We tried running the stem through the discs as a chord in v6. It was punchy but the stem read as a slash, not a spine. By v7 we had moved the stem outside the discs as a tangent, and rendered the lens with a clipPath instead of a hand-traced path. The clipPath was a small technical win: it meant the overlap was always mathematically exact, no matter how we moved the bowls.

v10: the equal-gap version we almost shipped

By v10 the proportions were locked. Disc ratio 5:4. Stem stroke 14, butt-capped for an architectural feel. The stem floated a few pixels off both bowls so the negative space between them read as a tiny triangle. We were ready to call it done.

Then we looked at it for a few days, and the floating stem started to bother us. A launchpad does not float. It leans on something. The triangle of negative space was beautiful, but it was also a way of saying that the spine and the bowls were not really part of the same object.

v11 to v12: making the stem lean

v11 closed both gaps. The stem became the common external tangent of the two unequal circles, kissing both at once. We solved a small system of equations to find the unique angle at which a stem anchored at the lower-left would touch both bowls simultaneously.

85 cos θ − 66 sin θ = R + 7 = 67   ⇒   θ ≈ 13.67°

That worked, but it made the bowls feel fused to the stem rather than supported by it. So v12 reopened a small gap on the bottom only, keeping the kiss on top. A few pixels of cream between spine and base. Breathing room.

We also switched from butt caps to round caps somewhere in here, which is a small change that does a lot. A round cap reads as drawn. A butt cap reads as cut. The mark needed to feel made, not stamped out.

v13: one shape, one magic

The last move was the simplest. We pushed the stem about five pixels into the upper bowl, and made the stem and the upper bowl the same colour. They stopped looking like two elements that happened to be near each other and started looking like one shape.

This was the moment the narrative clicked. The charcoal mass is the founder and the infrastructure as one thing, which is exactly the relationship BHAG Labs wants its founders to have with our tools. They are not separate. The lower bowl in terracotta is a different shape with a different colour. That is the market. The forest-green lens where the two shapes meet is product-market fit. The magic.

The math, since you asked

Every version sits on the same one-parameter family of geometry. Two unequal circles, a straight line. You can describe any version with two numbers: the overlap into the top disc (o) and the gap to the bottom disc (g).

80 sin θ − 7 cos θ = (R + 7 + g) − (r + 7 − o) = 17 + g + o

Plug in different o and g and you walk the entire design space.

  • v10: o = 0, g ≈ 6 on both sides. Stem floats.
  • v11: o = 0, g = 0. Stem tangent to both.
  • v12: o = 0, g = 4. Top tangent, bottom sliver.
  • v13: o = 5, g = 4. Overlap on top, sliver on bottom.

The dimensions came from one constraint we picked first: the bottom of the stem cap should land on the same horizontal line as the bottom of the lower disc. A B should terminate at a baseline like every other letter that terminates at a baseline. That fixed one degree of freedom and left us free to play with the other.

The palette

Four colours, doing different work.

#1a1a1a charcoal: founder + launchpad
#c1502e terracotta: the market
#2a4942 forest: product-market fit (light mode)
#c89b27 ochre: product-market fit (dark mode)
#f1ebe1 cream: the page

The lens is the only element that changes colour between light and dark mode. On cream, it is forest, which is grounded, slightly serious, the colour of a successful enterprise. On charcoal, it is ochre, which is warmer and reads as light against dark. Both colours mean the same thing in their context: this is where the value is.

What we actually learned

Three things, in case any of them help you with your own mark.

Iterate on the story before you iterate on the geometry. v1 through v9 were geometry problems. v10 looked correct and felt empty. v11 to v13 were narrative problems with geometric solutions. The order matters. Get the metaphor right and the inches start mattering. Try to get the inches right with no metaphor and you will produce a perfectly proportioned mark that says nothing.

Constraints are the design. We did not pick the disc ratio because it looked nice. We picked it because 5 / 4 matches classical B-bowl proportions, which means the mark inherits a quiet typographic familiarity. We did not pick the baseline-aligned cap because it looked nice. We picked it because every letter in the alphabet behaves that way. Each constraint we adopted from the outside (typography, classical geometry, Bauhaus restraint) saved us a decision and made the mark inherit a small piece of credibility.

The last move is usually the smallest. Five pixels of overlap between v12 and v13. One colour decision (paint the stem the same charcoal as the top disc). That was the change that made the mark mean something. Everything before it was setting the stage.

Where it lives

You will see this mark in a lot of places. As a favicon in every BHAG Labs product (Neev, Hissa, Pitchwala, Yantra, Bazaar, Runway). As an avatar on social. As the OG image on every link we share. As the watermark on every founder agreement we generate. We have published all the social and landscape formats here: /brand/social/

If you are a founder reading this, that mark is for you. The leaning stem is meant to be you. The bowls are what we are building so you have something to lean on. The lens is the bit we cannot promise, but we can shape the conditions for. Product-market fit is rare and we will not pretend otherwise. But it is more likely when the founder and the infrastructure are one shape.

Every big idea deserves a launchpad. Now ours has a face.
